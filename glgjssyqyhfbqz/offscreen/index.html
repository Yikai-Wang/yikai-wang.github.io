Let me start with what I mean by a world model. It has three parts. A hidden state that persists even when nobody's looking. A renderer, which turns that state into what you see. And dynamics, which carry the state forward when an action comes in.

This clip is from my recent work, FlashForward. The robot's arm swings past the crowd, and behind it, everything stays where it should. No sentence was needed for that.

[click] Language lives with the actor, outside the world. An LLM can drive the actor, with an instruction like “wave to the crowd”. But the state and its dynamics are visual. That's what I mean by a vision-centric world model.

How would we know that a model really holds a world? Here's the test I keep in mind. You look at a clock on the wall. It says ten past ten.

[click] Now you turn to the window for five minutes. The clock is out of view. But it doesn't wait for you. It keeps running.

[click] Then you look back. What should a model show you? On the left, the clock has vanished: no object permanence. In the middle, it's frozen where you left it: the model kept a memory, but a static one. On the right, it shows ten fifteen. That's a world model. It keeps going whether or not you're watching.

[click] This one small test gives us four requirements. Persist: the clock still exists while you're not looking. Consistent: it's the same clock, in the same place. Evolve: it keeps moving, because time passes. And act: if someone changes it, it stays changed. My past work explored each of these on its own. Today I'll focus on two of them at once: persist and evolve. The clock still exists, and it keeps running, while nobody looks. I call this off-screen evolution, and I think it's the next key problem for world models.

And today's models don't pass it. A benchmark at ECCV this year starts a process in front of the camera, hides it, and checks what comes back. Every model they tested, including Veo 3 and Sora 2 Pro, succeeds less than ten percent of the time. And every camera-controlled world model, Genie 3 included, scores zero.

Another benchmark, WorldMemBench, sends the camera back to the same place again and again. All nine models get worse with every return.

The pattern is the same everywhere. These models remember what things looked like. They don't know what things are doing now. The unseen world gets frozen.

Why does this matter? Because every agent lives like this. Here's a robot in a kitchen. Its camera sees one slice of the room: the cutting board in front of it. Everything else keeps going. The cup is filling. The pot is heating up. A ball rolls behind a box. Someone walks toward the robot. And the clock keeps running. None of that is in view, and all of it matters for what the robot should do next.

[click] It's the same for every agent. A robot needs to know the cup is about to overflow. A car needs to know there's a pedestrian behind that parked van. A wearable assistant should know you left the stove on. A world model that freezes the unseen hands its actor stale facts.

Today there are four answers. Static memory, like WorldMem, VMem, GEN3C or Genie 3, stores past views and copies them back. It's great for consistency, but the clock comes back frozen.

Retrieval methods like HyDRA and ReMind store the raw history and ask the generator to work out the change each time it reads. Pixel simulation, in LiveWorld and World Observer, keeps rendering the unseen part as video. That works, but the cost grows with every entity it watches. And EvoWorld and AnchorWorld let a language model write what happens off-screen. That tells you that something changed, not how much.

[click] My proposal sits in a new spot. Nothing is computed while the region is unseen. When it matters again, the model makes one jump by the elapsed time, and writes the result back.

So here's the core insight. The unseen world needs a state, not a simulation. Think about what you do yourself. While you look away, you don't watch the clock in your head. You just remember that it was running, and since when. When you look back, you work out ten fifteen in one step, with no simulation in between.

[click] My work on inpainting and occlusion, ASUKA, C2F-Seg and StreetUnveiler, showed that hidden is not gone. This proposal takes the next step: hidden is not paused.

Store the process, jump on demand

Here's how it works, with the clock. At time t-zero, the clock leaves view. The model stores a small visual state. Not just what the clock looks like, but what it's doing: it's running, at one turn per hour, it shows ten past ten, and it's stamped with the time. What it looks like goes to memory. What it's doing goes to the state.

[click] Then you look away for five minutes. And here's the key: nothing is computed. The state just sits there. No frames are rendered, and no simulation runs, however long you're away.

[click] When you look back, a learned jump operator, J, takes the stored state and the elapsed time, delta, and carries the state forward in a single step. Ten fifteen. Then the renderer draws it, with the clock's appearance from memory. It's one step whether you looked away for five seconds or five minutes.

[click] Finally, the jumped state is written back. So the next time you come back, the model starts from ten fifteen, not from the old frames. That's what keeps repeated revisits from drifting, which is exactly where today's models get worse.

Jump only when it matters

Revisit it comes back into view

Touched the actor reaches for it

Cost follows what you look back at, not the size of the world or the time away.

So when does the model jump? Only when it matters. Most of the world is static: the table, the plant. Those regions are never touched.

[click] First trigger: a revisit. The camera turns back to the clock, so the clock jumps forward.

[click] Second: something might walk into view on its own. Only regions with a moving process, like this person, get a cheap check every now and then.

[click] Third: the actor touches something. When the robot reaches for the cup, the cup jumps first. So the cost follows what you look back at. It doesn't grow with the size of the world, or with how long you looked away. That's what makes it practical for worlds that run for hours.

FlashForward already jumps

Same move. The gap is the time you looked away.

FlashForward · preprint 2026

This idea grows straight out of my own work. In FlashForward, the planner already jumps. It places clean anchors ten latents ahead, before any frame in between is rendered, and the renderer fills in the rest.

[click] Off-screen evolution is the same move. While the region is in view, we render as usual. When you look away, nothing is rendered. When you come back, one jump places a clean anchor at the right moment, and rendering picks up from there. The gap is simply the time you looked away. And the jump can be coarse: a few tokens carry where things are and how far they've got. Jump coarse, render fine. It's the same idea as the sub-goals in ProWAM, which jump ahead by task progress, not clock time.

Can we learn this without labels? Yes, because the answer key is already in video. First, any video with a time gap. Take the frames up to t-zero and predict the frame at t-zero plus delta. That trains the jump directly, from about a million clips, with no labels at all. Fixed cameras and time-lapses are ideal, because whatever changes between the two frames is the world, not the camera. And we add one simple rule: jumping two minutes and then three must land in the same place as jumping five at once. That way, the many short gaps in video also teach the model to make long jumps.

[click] Second, 360-degree and multi-camera video. We crop a viewport and move it around. The rest of the frame records exactly what happened off-screen, so we can supervise every moment of the look-away.

[click] Third, natural revisits in egocentric and robot video. When the camera comes back, the frame it sees is the answer key. And notice what's not needed: no text describes what happens off-screen. The dynamics come from vision.

How do we measure it? I propose the clock curve. Take one scene with a clock. Look away for one minute, five, ten, twenty, every time from the same start. When you look back, read the clock out of the frame, and plot it against how long you looked away.

The dashed line is the true time. A frozen model stays at ten-ten forever. A model that narrates in text knows that something changed, so it jumps once, but always by the same amount. Look away for ten minutes: the true clock says ten-twenty, the frozen model says ten-ten, and the narrated one says ten-fourteen.

[click] Our goal is a world model that stays on the dashed line, and keeps following it for look-aways longer than anything it saw in training. The clock test is one point on this curve. The clock curve is all of them.

[click] We sum it up as clock skill: how much of the frozen model's error we remove. Zero means no better than freezing, and one is perfect. And it isn't just clocks. It works for any process we can read out of a frame: a cup filling, a ball rolling, a person walking, the results of the robot's own actions, and even a still scene, where the right answer is no change at all.

The central experiment asks one question: how should the unseen be carried? We compare three ways, on the same backbone, with the same data and the same compute. Raw-history retrieval, which is where today's memory methods sit. Stepping the state at every frame, the classic filter. And storing the process, with a jump. On ordinary video, I expect all three to mostly freeze, because nothing there rewards keeping time.

[click] With time-gap and look-away data, the picture splits. Retrieval keeps time only as far as its context reaches. Stepping keeps time, but its cost grows with every second you're away. The jump keeps time at a flat cost. That's the result that matters most: the right structure, learned from the right data.

Around it, we also test look-aways longer than training, repeated revisits, things walking into view on their own, and language against vision. And we probe the stored state directly while it's hidden, so we know the model tracks the process, not just a plausible ending.

And then the part I care about most: acting. A world model is only as useful as the decisions it supports. So we build robot tasks where the important thing happens off-screen. Stop the tap before the cup overflows, while finishing another task. Find the ball that rolled behind a box. Catch the next item when the conveyor brings it into view.

We use the world model in two ways: as a planner that imagines sub-goals, the way ProWAM does, and as a training signal for the policy, the way DP4 does. The test is simple: does a model that keeps time beat the same model with frozen memory? That number tells us how much off-screen evolution matters for acting.

What could go wrong, and how we'll know

It learns to freeze

Most off-screen video is static.

Balance the data toward motion; probe the state while it's hidden.

Invisible processes

On or off, a kettle looks the same.

Reason from history, such as the action that switched it on.

Hidden things interact

The ball rolls into the vase.

Jump nearby movers together; check against frame-by-frame simulation.

Three things could get in the way, and each one has a clear check. First, freezing is attractive. Most off-screen video really is static, so a model can learn that nothing ever changes. We balance the data toward motion, and we probe the stored state while it's hidden, so we see it right away if it happens.

Second, some processes are invisible. A kettle that's on looks like one that's off. So we reason from history, such as the action that switched it on. If someone pressed the button a minute ago, the water is heating, even though nothing looks different.

Third, hidden things interact: the ball rolls into the vase. We jump nearby movers together, and we check against frame-by-frame simulation on exactly those scenes.

What I like about this plan is that every outcome tells us something. If plain memory also keeps time, the bottleneck is data, not the design, and we put everything into data. If frame-by-frame simulation clearly beats one jump, we simulate just the few moving things, at a coarse rate, and leave the static world alone.

If text gets the amounts right too, we add language to the state for slow changes. And if time-gap video alone is enough, we skip the curated data and simply scale.

Here's the plan, in three steps. We start with measurement, because the clock curve is useful on its own: it shows the whole field where models stand. So first, measure: a clock-curve benchmark, with synthetic scenes where we know the exact state, and real 360-degree and multi-camera clips. Second, learn: the jump model, learned from ordinary video, without labels. Third, apply it wherever the unseen matters: robots, driving, wearable assistants, games and simulators.
